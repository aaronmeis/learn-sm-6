1. First 60 seconds (spoken script)

"We request executive authorization to prioritize enterprise capital allocation for multi-domain SM-6 kill-chain integration and network assurance over standalone missile procurement across naval, air, and land platforms.

Because SM-6 rounds require a 36-to-39-month production lead time at a current then-year unit cost of approximately $4.3M, procuring isolated effectors without guaranteeing the surrounding sensor-to-shooter network creates 'blind rounds.' Without offboard fire-control queuing, these effectors are restricted to organic shipboard radar horizons of 20 to 30 nautical miles, leaving our extended-range air defense, terminal ballistic missile defense, and maritime strike capabilities tethered to localized line-of-sight limits while consuming finite Mk 41 VLS magazine capacity.

3. Five killer questions (20-second answers)

1. Why now?

Spoken Answer: SM-6 rounds require a 36-to-39-month production lead time, meaning decisions today directly dictate fleet magazine depth in FY2027, when requested procurement expands to 540 rounds. Furthermore, deployment across Army Typhon and air-launched AIM-174B platforms creates immediate multi-domain demand that requires synchronized kill-web investment right now.

Elevate (Strategic Implication): Lead-time inertia means delaying procurement today guarantees fleet magazine exhaustion during peak strategic vulnerability windows three years out.

2. What if we wait? (Dated cost of no)

Spoken Answer: If we wait, shipboard VLS magazines will face severe inventory exhaustion as high-end effectors are expended without timely replenishment. Surface forces will be forced to collapse their engagement footprint back to organic radar horizons (~20–30 nautical miles), leaving multi-billion-dollar capital assets exposed to unmitigated terminal MRBM and hypersonic threat profiles by FY2027.

Elevate (Strategic Implication): Delaying exchanges an assured offboard defense network for localized, high-risk organic engagements that put surface combatants and strike groups at catastrophic risk.

3. Who else has done this? (Benchmark or analogue)

Spoken Answer: Allied forces and sister services are already executing this transition. Australia actively participated in the FTM-32 live MRBM intercept in March 2024; Japan secured State Department approval for a $900M SM-6 FMS buy in January 2025; and the U.S. Army deployed ground-based Typhon launchers to the Philippines in April 2024, successfully sinking a maritime target during Talisman Sabre 2025.

Elevate (Strategic Implication): Joint and coalition adoption validates SM-6 as the Pacific theater standard for integrated air, missile, and sea-denial operations.

4. What are we stopping? (Capacity / tradeoff)

Spoken Answer: We are stopping the doctrine of expending high-cost SM-6 interceptors (~$4.3M per round) against low-tier, low-cost threats like basic UAVs or subsonic cruise missiles. We are enforcing strict layered magazine allocation, preserving SM-6 VLS cells for terminal BMD and over-the-horizon strikes while offloading air-breathing defense to ESSM Block II and SM-2.

Elevate (Strategic Implication): Restricting effector usage based on threat tier preserves finite VLS magazine depth and defeats favorable adversary cost-imposition strategies.

5. What if this slips? (Risk and recovery)

Spoken Answer: If the developmental Block IB rocket motor program slips further following its FY2026 strategic pause, we recover operational capability by executing software and electronics upgrades (SWUP / Block IAU) on mature, fielded Block I/IA airframes. This preserves terminal BMD and extended-range air defense enhancements without exposing fleet stockpiles to propulsion qualification delays.

Elevate (Strategic Implication): Decoupling software-driven mission growth from mechanical rocket motor redesigns ensures continuous capability insertion even if propulsion programs stall.
